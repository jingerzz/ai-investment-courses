# Conversation Guide: Starter Prompts

Example prompts to use in your Stock Research project. Each one
shows a specific technique from the reading. Try them out, then
adapt them for companies you're interested in.

---

## Getting Started

These are good opening messages for a new conversation.

**Set your focus:**
```
I want to learn about Netflix's business. Start with how they
make money, then tell me who their biggest competitors are.
```
*Why it works:* Tells Claude exactly what you want and in what
order. You get a structured answer instead of a wall of text.

**Ask Claude to explain a concept:**
```
Explain what a P/E ratio is. Use Apple as an example. Keep it
simple -- I'm in high school and just learning this.
```
*Why it works:* Gives Claude your level and asks for a specific
example. You'll get a clear explanation, not a textbook definition.

---

## Comparing Companies

These prompts ask Claude to analyze and compare.

**Side-by-side comparison:**
```
Compare Apple and Nike as investments. For each one, give me:
what the company does in one sentence, one reason it's a good
investment, and one risk. Put it in a table.
```
*Why it works:* The structure (one sentence, one positive, one
risk, table format) keeps Claude focused. You get something you
can actually use for your notes or stock club.

**Growth comparison:**
```
Between Disney and Netflix, which company do you think has more
room to grow over the next five years? Give me three reasons
for your pick and one reason the other company might win instead.
```
*Why it works:* Asking for reasons (not just an answer) and
including the counterargument forces Claude to think through both
sides.

**Teen-relevant angle:**
```
Which of these companies is most popular with teenagers right
now: Apple, Nike, Tesla, Disney, or Netflix? How does teen
popularity translate into stock performance? Does it even matter?
```
*Why it works:* Connects something you actually know about (which
brands your friends use) to investing concepts. Claude can bridge
the gap between your experience and financial analysis.

## Working with Documents

Use these after you've uploaded an article or document to your
project.

**Key takeaways:**
```
Summarize the article I uploaded in three bullet points. What's
the most important thing an investor should know from this?
```
*Why it works:* Three bullet points forces Claude to prioritize.
The investor angle means it won't just summarize the news -- it'll
tell you why it matters for the stock.

**Find what matters:**
```
Based on the article I uploaded, is this good news or bad news
for the company's stock? Explain your reasoning.
```
*Why it works:* Simple question, but it requires Claude to
connect news to stock implications -- which is exactly the skill
you're building.

**Go deeper:**
```
What questions should I research next based on this article?
Give me three things I should look up to understand the full
picture.
```
*Why it works:* Instead of just consuming information, you're
asking Claude to help you figure out what you don't know yet.
This builds your research skills.

## Pushing Back and Iterating

Use these after Claude gives you an initial answer. They make
the second response better than the first.

**Challenge a missing factor:**
```
You didn't mention competition. How does [competitor] change
the picture for [company]?
```
*Why it works:* You're bringing your own knowledge into the
conversation. Claude will incorporate it and give you a better
analysis.

**Ask for the other side:**
```
That sounds like the positive case. Now give me the bear case
-- what could go wrong for this company?
```
*Why it works:* Pushes Claude past the easy answer. Good investors
always consider what could go wrong, not just what could go right.

**Check for hallucinations:**
```
You mentioned [specific fact]. I want to verify that before I
trust it. How confident are you, and where should I look it up
myself?
```
*Why it works:* Practices the verification habit from the reading.
Don't ask "What's your source?" -- Claude might invent a fake
source. Instead, ask where *you* can verify it. Claude will
sometimes admit uncertainty when challenged this way, which tells
you the original claim wasn't reliable.

**Simplify:**
```
That was too complicated. Explain it again like I'm explaining
it to a friend who knows nothing about stocks.
```
*Why it works:* If you can't understand the answer, it's not
useful. Don't be afraid to ask for a simpler version. That's not
a weakness -- that's good prompting.
